Key terms & formula sheet

All the formulas from every lesson in one place. The lesson sections below refer back to these.

Key terms

Antiderivative
\(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) if \(F'(x)=f(x)\). Integrating means finding an antiderivative.
Indefinite integral
\(\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\). The \(\int\) sign is the integral sign, \(f(x)\) is the integrand, and \(dx\) names the variable of integration.
Constant of integration \(C\)
Any two antiderivatives differ by a constant, so always write \(+C\). \(F(x)\) alone is called the particular integral.
u-substitution
Let \(u=g(x)\), so \(du=g'(x)\,dx\). This turns \(\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx\) into \(\int f(u)\,du\).
Correction factor
A constant multiplied inside the integral to complete \(du\), with its reciprocal placed outside. It only works for constants, never variables.
Improper rational function
The numerator's degree is ≥ the denominator's. Do long division first. A proper one has a lower numerator degree.
Integration by parts (IBP)
\(\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\). Use it for products of different function types. Pick u with LIATE: Log, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential.
Tabular method
Repeated IBP set up as a table. Differentiate the polynomial down to 0, integrate the other factor, then multiply along the diagonals with signs \(+,-,+,\dots\)
Algebraic substitution
Removes radicals. \(x=z^n\) (n = LCD of the fractional exponents) or \(ax+b=z^n\).
Trigonometric substitution
\(a^2-u^2\to u=a\sin\theta\), \(a^2+u^2\to u=a\tan\theta\), \(u^2-a^2\to u=a\sec\theta\). Convert back with a reference triangle.
Completing the square
Rewrites \(ax^2+bx+c\) as \(\pm(u^2\pm a^2)\) so an inverse-trig or log form applies.
Partial fractions
Splits a proper rational function into simple fractions over the factors of the denominator: linear, repeated, or irreducible quadratic.

Rewriting rules the "why" behind most steps

Reciprocal identities

\[\sec\theta=\frac1{\cos\theta}\qquad\csc\theta=\frac1{\sin\theta}\qquad\cot\theta=\frac1{\tan\theta}\]

These are definitions: sec, csc and cot are just 1 over cos, sin and tan. Squaring keeps them true, so \(\frac1{\cos^2\theta}=\left(\frac1{\cos\theta}\right)^2=\sec^2\theta\). Pairs to remember: sin ↔ csc, cos ↔ sec, tan ↔ cot.

Quotient identities

\[\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\qquad\cot\theta=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\]

On the unit circle, sin is the height and cos is the width, so tan is height ÷ width (the slope). Combined with the reciprocals: \(\frac{\sin x}{\cos^2x}=\frac1{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\sec x\tan x\).

Derivatives you need for du

\[(\sin u)'=\cos u\quad(\cos u)'=-\sin u\quad(\tan u)'=\sec^2u\]\[(\cot u)'=-\csc^2u\quad(\sec u)'=\sec u\tan u\quad(\csc u)'=-\csc u\cot u\]\[(e^u)'=e^u\qquad(a^u)'=a^u\ln a\qquad(\ln u)'=\frac1u\]

Every "find du" step uses one of these. Chain rule: multiply by the derivative of the inside, so \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin3x=3\cos3x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{x^2}=2xe^{x^2}\).

Negative & fractional exponents

\[\frac1{a^n}=a^{-n}\qquad\sqrt[n]{a^m}=a^{m/n}\]

A power in the denominator moves to the top with its sign flipped, and back again. A root is a fractional power: square root = power ½, cube root = power ⅓. Example: \(\frac1{\sqrt x}=x^{-1/2}\).

Exponent laws

\[a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}\qquad\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\qquad(a^m)^n=a^{mn}\]

Multiplying adds the exponents, dividing subtracts them, and a power of a power multiplies them. Example: \(\frac{x^2}{x^{1/2}}=x^{2-\frac12}=x^{3/2}\).

e and ln

\[e^{\ln u}=u\qquad\ln e^u=u\qquad k\ln x=\ln x^k\]

\(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) are inverse functions, so one undoes the other. The log power rule moves a number in front of ln up into the exponent.

Fraction moves

\[a\div\frac bc=a\cdot\frac cb\qquad\frac{a+b}{c}=\frac ac+\frac bc\]

Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its flip. A sum on top can be split into separate fractions, but a sum on the bottom cannot.

Algebra moves

\[a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)\qquad(a\pm b)^2=a^2\pm2ab+b^2\]\[x^2+bx=\left(x+\tfrac b2\right)^2-\tfrac{b^2}4\]

Difference of squares, expanding a square, and completing the square: take half the x-coefficient, square it, then add and subtract it.

Formula sheet

Basic rules & power formula L1

  1. \[\int dx=x+C\]

    Integral of 1. Integrating plain \(dx\) gives the variable itself. Every answer gets \(+C\), because the derivative of any constant is 0. Example: \(\int dt=t+C\).

  2. \[\int cf\,dx=c\int f\,dx\]

    Constant multiple rule. Move a number out front, integrate, then multiply it back in. Example: \(\int5x^2dx=5\cdot\frac{x^3}3=\frac53x^3+C\).

  3. \[\int(f_1\pm f_2)\,dx=\int f_1\,dx\pm\int f_2\,dx\]

    Sum and difference rule. Integrate one term at a time. Example: \(\int(x^2+3)\,dx=\frac{x^3}3+3x+C\).

  4. \[\int u^n\,du=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\quad n\neq-1\]

    Power formula. Add 1 to the exponent, then divide by the new exponent. It fails for \(n=-1\) (you'd divide by 0), and that case gives \(\ln|u|\) instead (Lesson 2). \(u\) can be plain \(x\) or an inner function, as long as \(du\) is there. Examples: \(\int x^4dx=\frac{x^5}5+C\), \(\int x^{-1/2}dx=2x^{1/2}+C\).

Logarithm L2

  1. \[\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln|u|+C\]

    Log rule. If the top of a fraction is the derivative of the bottom, the answer is ln of the bottom. Fix a missing constant with a correction factor. The absolute value is there because ln only accepts positive numbers. This is the \(n=-1\) case the power formula can't handle. Example: \(\int\frac{2x\,dx}{x^2+1}=\ln(x^2+1)+C\).

Exponential L3

  1. \[\int e^u\,du=e^u+C\]

    Base e. \(e\) to a power integrates to itself. Just divide by the constant that comes from \(du\). Example: \(\int e^{3x}dx=\frac13e^{3x}+C\).

  2. \[\int a^u\,du=\frac{a^u}{\ln a}+C\]

    Any other base. Same idea, then also divide by \(\ln a\). Example: \(\int2^x\,dx=\frac{2^x}{\ln2}+C\).

  3. \[e^{\ln u}=u\qquad a^{\log_a x}=x\qquad \ln e^u=u\]

    Simplify first. e and ln cancel each other. Example: \(e^{2\ln x}=e^{\ln x^2}=x^2\).

Trig, basic six L4

  1. \[\int\sin u\,du=-\cos u\qquad\int\cos u\,du=\sin u\]

    Sine and cosine. These are derivative rules read backwards. Watch the minus sign on sine. Example: \(\int\sin2x\,dx=-\frac12\cos2x+C\).

  2. \[\int\sec^2u\,du=\tan u\qquad\int\csc^2u\,du=-\cot u\]

    Squares of sec and csc. These come from the derivatives of tan and cot. Example: \(\int\sec^23x\,dx=\frac13\tan3x+C\).

  3. \[\int\sec u\tan u\,du=\sec u\qquad\int\csc u\cot u\,du=-\csc u\]

    Pairs. These come from the derivatives of sec and csc. Example: \(\int\sec2x\tan2x\,dx=\frac12\sec2x+C\).

Tip: an inside angle \(ax\) always adds a factor of \(\frac1a\) in front.

Trig, log type L4

  1. \[\int\tan u\,du=\ln|\sec u|=-\ln|\cos u|\]\[\int\cot u\,du=\ln|\sin u|\]

    Tangent and cotangent. These come from the log rule, because \(\tan u=\frac{\sin u}{\cos u}\) and the top is (minus) the derivative of the bottom. Example: \(\int\tan2x\,dx=\frac12\ln|\sec2x|+C\).

  2. \[\int\sec u\,du=\ln|\sec u+\tan u|\]\[\int\csc u\,du=\ln|\csc u-\cot u|\]

    Secant and cosecant. There's no easy derivation, so memorize these two. Example: \(\int\sec3x\,dx=\frac13\ln|\sec3x+\tan3x|+C\).

Identities you'll need

  1. \[\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\quad 1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\quad 1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\]

    Pythagorean. Swap between sin and cos, tan and sec, or cot and csc. Example: \(\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1\), so \(\int\tan^2x\,dx=\tan x-x+C\).

  2. \[\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\qquad \cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\]

    Double angle. Turns a product into a single function. Example: \(\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin2x\).

  3. \[\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)\qquad \cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\]

    Half-angle (power reducing). Gets rid of a square so you can integrate. Example: \(\int\sin^2x\,dx=\frac x2-\frac14\sin2x+C\).

Inverse trig forms L5, a > 0

  1. \[\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{a^2-u^2}}=\arcsin\frac ua+C\]

    Arcsin. Square root of (constant − variable²) in the denominator. Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}=\arcsin\frac x2+C\).

  2. \[\int\frac{du}{a^2+u^2}=\frac1a\arctan\frac ua+C\]

    Arctan. Constant + variable² in the denominator, with no root. Don't forget the \(\frac1a\) in front. Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{9+x^2}=\frac13\arctan\frac x3+C\).

  3. \[\int\frac{du}{u\sqrt{u^2-a^2}}=\frac1a\operatorname{arcsec}\frac{|u|}a+C\]

    Arcsec. u times the root of (variable² − constant). Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}}=\operatorname{arcsec}|x|+C\).

How to read a and u: \(a^2\) is the constant part and \(u^2\) is the variable part. Take square roots to get a and u.

Log / inverse-hyperbolic forms L5

  1. \[\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2\pm a^2}}=\ln\left(u+\sqrt{u^2\pm a^2}\right)+C\]

    Root of u² ± a². The variable comes first under the root, so it's not arcsin. Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}=\ln\left(x+\sqrt{x^2+1}\right)+C\).

  2. \[\int\frac{du}{a^2-u^2}=\frac1{2a}\ln\left|\frac{a+u}{a-u}\right|+C\]

    a² − u², no root. A minus sign instead of plus, so it's not arctan. Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{1-x^2}=\frac12\ln\left|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right|+C\).

  3. \[\int\frac{du}{u\sqrt{a^2\pm u^2}}=-\frac1a\ln\frac{a+\sqrt{a^2\pm u^2}}{|u|}+C\]

    u times root of a² ± u². The constant comes first, so it's not arcsec. Example: \(\int\frac{dx}{x\sqrt{1+x^2}}=-\ln\frac{1+\sqrt{1+x^2}}{|x|}+C\).

Compare with the inverse trig forms: the order and sign inside decide which formula applies.

LESSON 1

Antiderivatives & the Power Formula

Undo differentiation. Rewrite roots and fractions as powers, then use \(\int u^n\,du=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\). In u-substitution, the rest of the integrand must be \(du\), up to a constant.

  1. Rewrite: \(\sqrt[n]{x^m}=x^{m/n}\), \(\frac1{x^n}=x^{-n}\). Expand simple products.
  2. Look for an inner function \(u\) whose derivative appears as a factor.
  3. Write \(du\). If a constant is missing, add a correction factor (\(k\) inside, \(\frac1k\) outside).
  4. Apply the power formula, substitute back, and add \(C\).

Formula practice · basic rules

B1

\(\displaystyle\int7\,dx\)

rules 1 + 2

Uses∫dx = x Constant multiple

  1. Spot the rules: 7 is a constant multiplied by \(dx\).
  2. Move the constant out (rule 2): \(\int7\,dx=7\int dx\).
  3. Integrate (rule 1): \(\int dx=x\), so \(7\int dx=7x\).
  4. Add C: \(7x+C\).
  5. Check: \(\frac{d}{dx}(7x)=7\) ✓

Why +C? Integrating asks "what function has this derivative?", and there are infinitely many answers. \(7x\), \(7x+3\) and \(7x-10\) all have derivative 7, because the derivative of a constant is 0. Differentiating erases any constant, so integrating can't tell which one was there. \(+C\) stands for "plus any constant" and covers all of them at once. Leaving it out is usually marked wrong.
When it's required: on every indefinite integral (no limits), which covers everything in this reviewer. When it isn't: definite integrals with limits like \(\int_1^3\), because the C cancels when you subtract, and the \(v\) you find in integration by parts.

Answer\(7x+C\)

\(\displaystyle\int x^6\,dx\)

rule 4

UsesPower formula

  1. Identify n: \(x^6\) has exponent \(n=6\).

B3

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{x^3}\)

rule 4, negative power

UsesPower formula

  1. Rewrite as a power: \(\frac1{x^3}=x^{-3}\), so \(n=-3\).Why? A power in the denominator moves to the top with a negative exponent, \(\frac1{a^n}=a^{-n}\), and back again. See the rule
  2. Add 1 to the exponent: \(-3+1=-2\), giving \(x^{-2}\).
  3. Divide by the new exponent: \(\frac{x^{-2}}{-2}\).
  4. Clean up: move \(x^{-2}\) back to the denominator: \(\frac{x^{-2}}{-2}=-\frac1{2x^2}\).
  5. Check: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(-\frac12x^{-2}\right)=-\frac12\cdot(-2)x^{-3}=x^{-3}\) ✓

Answer\(-\dfrac1{2x^2}+C\)

B4

\(\displaystyle\int\sqrt[3]{x}\,dx\)

rule 4, fractional power

UsesPower formula

  1. Rewrite as a power: \(\sqrt[3]x=x^{1/3}\), so \(n=\frac13\).Why? A root is a fractional power: \(\sqrt[n]{a^m}=a^{m/n}\). A square root is the power ½. See the rule
  2. Add 1 to the exponent: \(\frac13+1=\frac13+\frac33=\frac43\), giving \(x^{4/3}\).
  3. Divide by the new exponent: \(\frac{x^{4/3}}{4/3}\).
  4. Clean up the fraction: to divide by a fraction, flip it and multiply: \[\frac{x^{4/3}}{\tfrac43}=x^{4/3}\times\frac34=\frac34x^{4/3}\] Same idea with numbers: \(6\div\frac12=6\times2=12\).Why? Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its flip: \(\div\frac ab=\times\frac ba\). See the rule
  5. Check: \(\frac{d}{dx}\frac34x^{4/3}=\frac34\cdot\frac43x^{1/3}=x^{1/3}\) ✓

Answer\(\frac34x^{4/3}+C\)

B5

\(\displaystyle\int(3x^2-4x+7)\,dx\)

all 4 rules

Uses∫dx = x Constant multiple Sum rule Power formula

  1. Split into terms (rule 3): \(\int3x^2dx-\int4x\,dx+\int7\,dx\).Why? A sum on top can be split into separate fractions, \(\frac{a+b}c=\frac ac+\frac bc\), and the sum rule lets you integrate each piece separately. See the rule
  2. Pull out the constants (rule 2): \(3\int x^2dx-4\int x\,dx+7\int dx\).
  3. Power formula on each (rule 4): \(\int x^2dx=\frac{x^3}3\), \(\int x\,dx=\frac{x^2}2\), \(\int dx=x\).
  4. Multiply back: \(3\cdot\frac{x^3}3-4\cdot\frac{x^2}2+7x=x^3-2x^2+7x\).
  5. Add one C at the end: \(x^3-2x^2+7x+C\).

B6

\(\displaystyle\int(x+1)(x-2)\,dx\)

expand first

UsesSum rule Power formula

  1. No product rule for integrals: you can't integrate each factor separately, so multiply it out first.
  2. Expand (FOIL): \((x+1)(x-2)=x^2-2x+x-2=x^2-x-2\).Why? Expanding means multiplying every term in one bracket by every term in the other, e.g. \((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\). See the rule
  3. Integrate term by term: \(\frac{x^3}3-\frac{x^2}2-2x\).
  4. Add C.

B8

\(\displaystyle\int(2x+5)^4\,dx\)

rule 4 with u

UsesPower formula u-substitution Correction factor

  1. Why substitute? The power formula is \(\int u^4\,du\). The thing raised to the power has to match the d-part. Here the base is \((2x+5)\), but we only have \(dx\), not \(d(2x+5)\). Expanding \((2x+5)^4\) would also work, but it's long.
  2. Name the inside: let \(u=2x+5\).Why? u-substitution: pick u as the inside piece whose derivative also shows up in the integral (up to a constant). Then everything can be written in terms of u. See the rule
  3. Differentiate both sides: \(\frac{du}{dx}=2\) (the 2x gives 2, and the constant 5 gives 0). Multiply both sides by dx: \(du=2\,dx\).

LESSON 2

Integrals Yielding Natural Logarithms

\(\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln|u|+C\). If the numerator is the derivative of the denominator, up to a constant, the answer is the log of the denominator.

  1. If the numerator's degree is ≥ the denominator's, do long division first.
  2. Let \(u\) = denominator and compute \(du\). Fix any constant with a correction factor.
  3. If the numerator only partly matches, split it into (multiple of \(du\)) + (leftover). See example 5.

LESSON 3

Integration of Exponential Functions

\(\int e^u\,du=e^u+C\) and \(\int a^u\,du=\frac{a^u}{\ln a}+C\). The exponent is your u, so make sure its derivative is in the integrand.

  1. Simplify first: \(e^{\ln x^3}=x^3\), \(\ln e^{2x}=2x\), \(\sqrt{a^u}=a^{u/2}\). Expand squares and split fractions over \(e^x\).
  2. Let \(u\) = the exponent, compute \(du\), and fix the constant.
  3. Base \(e\): the answer is the same function. Any other base \(a\): divide by \(\ln a\).

LESSON 5

Integrals Leading to Inverse Trig Functions

Match the integral to one of the three inverse trig forms. Identify \(a^2\) (a constant) and \(u^2\) (the variable part), then check that \(du\) is present.

  1. Find \(a^2\) and \(u^2\). Take square roots to get \(a\) and \(u\), then compute \(du\) and fix the constant.
  2. For a quadratic \(ax^2+bx+c\), complete the square first.
  3. If the numerator has an \(x\) term, split it into (a multiple of the derivative of the quadratic) + (a constant). The first part gives \(\ln\), the second gives \(\arctan\) or \(\arcsin\).

LESSON 7

Integration by Parts

\(\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\). Choose \(u\) so that it gets simpler when differentiated, and \(dv\) so it's easy to integrate. LIATE tells you which factor to make \(u\).

  1. Pick \(u\) by LIATE (whichever comes first: Log, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential). The rest is \(dv\).
  2. Compute \(du\) and \(v\) (no \(+C\) needed for \(v\)).
  3. Write \(uv-\int v\,du\). Repeat if the new integral still needs parts. The tabular method is faster for polynomial × (sin, cos, or \(e^{x}\)).
  4. Cycling integrals (\(e^x\sin x\), \(\sec^3x\)): when the original integral \(I\) shows up again, move it to the left and solve, e.g. \(2I=\dots\)

LESSON 9

Integration by Substitution

Algebraic substitution gets rid of radicals: \(x=z^n\) (n = LCD of the exponents) or \(ax+b=z^n\). Trig substitution handles \(a^2\pm u^2\) and \(u^2-a^2\) using the table in the formula sheet. After a trig substitution, use a reference triangle to convert θ back to x.

  1. Choose the substitution and write x and dx in terms of the new variable.
  2. Simplify the integrand. If you get an improper fraction in z, use long division.
  3. Integrate, then substitute back. For trig substitution, read sinθ, cosθ, tanθ off the triangle.

  1. Remove the root: let \(x=z^2\), so \(\sqrt x=z\) and \(dx=2z\,dz\).Why? Algebraic substitution: set the root (or the expression under it) equal to a new variable so the radical disappears, then rewrite x and dx in terms of it. See the rule

LESSON 10

Partial Fractions

Break a proper rational function into simple fractions, one group per factor of the denominator. Then each piece integrates to a \(\ln\), a power, or an \(\arctan\).

  1. If it's improper, do long division first.
  2. Factor the denominator completely and write the forms from the partial fractions table.
  3. Multiply through by the denominator. Plug in the roots to get some constants quickly, then equate coefficients for the rest.
  4. Integrate each piece. For a quadratic piece \(\frac{A(2ax+b)+B}{ax^2+bx+c}\), the A part gives \(A\ln\), and the B part gives an arctan after completing the square.

UsesPartial fractions table Log rule

  1. Factor the bottom: \(2x^2-3x-2=(2x+1)(x-2)\).
  2. Set up the pieces (two linear factors): \(\frac{8x-1}{(2x+1)(x-2)}=\frac A{2x+1}+\frac B{x-2}\).Why?
